- Use controlled muting on the hi-hat channel, but control this muting with the mic closest to the snare drum. Make the gate attack minimal (super fast) and the release time just long enough to recover before the next hi-hat hit. Range - -10…-20dB.
- Example. Let's say we rotated the microphone 45 degrees off axis, which caused a 10dB reduction in treble at 10kHz. The microphone now looks into empty space - into the vibrating "air" of the cabinet. If we now add 10dB at 10kHz with the EQ, the guitar will sound normal again, just with the extra sensitivity to vibration, openness, and gain. Note: When rotating any microphone for this purpose, remember that you are rotating the microphone pattern to ensure that the pattern is capturing the “chunk of air” you need.
- Here's an analogy: adding 20dB at 80Hz to white noise will boost that frequency range, but not turn it into a sine. It won't make your hair shake or your neck or your soul 80 times a second. But by placing the mic where the strong 80Hz section is, you will make the world tremble at that frequency. Boosting the treble will also boost the 160, 320, 640 Hz harmonics, making the bass feel more pronounced in small speakers. So let your microphone contribute to that deep resonance.
- The first thing to do is to set the ratio as large as possible (20:1, infinity, etc.) - in short, the maximum. Next is the release to the fastest option, which is likely to be faster than you need. Next, feed the audio signal to the module, either by lowering the threshold or by increasing the input gain (depending on the compressor design), and listen while adjusting only the attack. Listen for the attack - the rising edge of the signal - as you turn the attack knob. Try to ignore the horrendous dips caused by the release's post-effects - listen only to the attack. (A super-fast release gives you the opportunity to hear many more individual attacks than a slow one). Listen for the rising edge of the signal. Determine how the attack affects the size of the burst. So if you compress the snare and attack at minimum time, it's like leaving only the bare snap of a drumstick.
- Think of the ratio control like the lenses of a telephoto lens - the larger the ratio, the smaller the sound, although it may be more controllable. Reducing the ratio to 2:1 (while delivering the same voltage) is aesthetically similar to a larger picture. The smaller the ratio, the larger it is - with the risk of going beyond the acceptable limits. And vice versa - with the growth of the relationship, it becomes smaller, but within a stricter framework. The idea is usually to try to make it bigger, but keep it within the proper limits. So lower the ratio until you can't hear the effects you like - the thickness of the click, the groove created by the release - you can raise the ratio a bit by constantly controlling the size of the picture. At this point, don't think about attack and release, and don't think about release in terms of numbers - think about the size and signature of the sound. Did you count how many times I mentioned "brand" and "hardness factor"? So, raising the ratio, the sound becomes more firm (and smaller), and when lowering - softer (but more). So you might be thinking "How branded do I want to make this?" instead of "How big do I want to make this?".
- Warning! What many engineers don't realize is that compression ratios are multiplied, not added. If you compress a mix 10:1 and then the mastering engineer compresses it 10:1, you end up with 100:1 instead of 20:1. Consider yourself forewarned. This applies to any compression. If you press 10:1 and then 4:1, you don't have 14:1, but 40:1. The next time you hit a mix, consider the ratios commonly used on radio stations as a last resort. Ask yourself "how small can I create a sound to send to ether?".
- You have been monitoring at too high a volume, forcing amplifiers, monitors and ears "swallow" many bursts, giving you a false sense of their space. These unnoticed spikes will also give you a false impression of the perceived loudness of the product. Sharp bursts, limited in the mastering, will cut the volume of resonant components in your mix by tens of decibels, making the whole product quieter, but you will never hear those sharp bursts listening to material at 110dB SPL! The mastering engineer will do a real check and suppress these bursts in the process.
- If your 0VU is set to -18dB peak, you can afford not to worry about dynamic range, but you can pay for it at the mastering stage when someone wants to compress your material. A level of -18dB means that even a sloppy engineer is unlikely to overdrive. On the other hand, if your 0VUs are equivalent to -12dB, you should definitely keep an eye on the level and work extremely hard on your psychoacoustic goal of achieving Maximum Illusion at Minimum Voltage (our goal in life, remember?). Setting zero to -12dB is like taking the game to the next level. When your gain settings are done like this, you'll quickly learn to control those annoying distortions that prevent your instruments from recording at their best at maximum digital resolution.
- A combination of VU meters and peak meters is required. In the digital space you want a higher VU to peak ratio than analog. If you manage to increase the VU without increasing the peaks - that's cool! This means that you have achieved more effective volume. If You received climb peaks without increase in VU - This just a waste of energy. Over time, you will be able to raise your 0VU bar from -18dB to -12dB! By comparison, in the past, when using analog recorders, a skilled engineer would tend to increase the ratio of the highest peak to the 0VU level. The peak could penetrate the blurry area of focus without distorting the VU-sensitive components. We know that analog systems (such as film) absorb peak bursts by half, so it took a lot of work to create powerful bursts. In digital studios, this approach backfires. VU ratio to the peak is difficult to measure without VU meters and peaks. Even the most experienced engineer will benefit from both of these tools.
- Trying each track on a single “voltage”, the subconscious mind will remember the Illusion / Voltage ratio for each track. There is no need to make every sound jump out of the speaker with volume and presence. This approach will rob your mix of depth and create a sound made up of small, weightless sounds. As you go through the sounds, you will notice that with equal voltage, some sounds are louder and some are quieter. Remember : only "high-voltage" instruments limit the sound level of the mix on the final CD. The ones that shine through the mix, sitting at -10dB, are already more efficient on their own, and therefore don't raise the overall volume, as they don't need to lift themselves. All you need to do is simply lower them down just enough to achieve musical balance.
- Most engineers plug in an EQ, crank the gain all the way up, and start turning the knobs preoccupiedly looking for a frequency that will make the hair on the back of the neck move. The big problem with this approach is that for every correct frequency you hear, you're presented with a hundred incorrect ones, and even with a spread of 15dB. Psychoacoustically, this has the effect of dulling your hearing when trying to cope with a set of jumpy frequencies. This results in a "played but not won" effect on the EQ issue, with poor perception (bad combination), which explains the lack of accuracy and coherence resulting from such an approach.
- At this point, you will be comparing the EQ settings to your imagination, and accurately You will know where you hit and where you missed. This method will quickly teach your ears how, for example, 3kHz really sounds. When you turn on the EQ, you will think: "Hehe, I thought 3kHz was higher" or "Oh shit, I meant a higher frequency!".
- If you EQ all your instruments solo, you usually use your favorite frequency. This creates a sound that masks that frequency on all instruments. For example, if you like the frequency of 8 kHz, and you add it everywhere, then not only will you degrade instrument separation at that frequency, but you will also create relative attenuation above 8kHz, creating a muddier, less bright overall mix.

- Secret scheme: cut everything below 1 or 2kHz, then max out a thin band at 14kHz, and send that signal to the limiter's sidechain input.
